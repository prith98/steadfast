The geological record preserves a long and discontinuous account of the surface of the planet. Sedimentary rocks accumulate in basins where water, wind, or ice transport eroded material from elevated regions toward lower-lying terrain. Over time, the accumulated sediment is compacted by the weight of subsequent layers, and chemical processes cement the grains together. The resulting strata can persist for hundreds of millions of years, and the relative order of the layers, with older beds beneath younger ones, is the foundation of stratigraphic dating. Cross-cutting relationships, in which an intrusion or fault postdates the rock it interrupts, refine the ordering further.

Plate tectonics describes the slow horizontal motion of the lithosphere, the rigid outer shell of the planet, as it rides on the underlying asthenosphere. Convective motion in the mantle drives this circulation, and the boundaries between plates concentrate most of the world's seismic and volcanic activity. Divergent boundaries occur where plates pull apart, allowing magma to rise and form new oceanic crust along mid-ocean ridges. Convergent boundaries form where one plate is forced beneath another, producing deep trenches, mountain ranges, and arc volcanism. Transform boundaries occur where plates slide past one another laterally, accumulating strain that is released in earthquakes when the friction along the contact is finally overcome.

The hydrological cycle moves water between reservoirs over a wide range of timescales. Evaporation from open water and transpiration from vegetation lift moisture into the atmosphere, where it cools and condenses into cloud droplets. Precipitation returns water to the surface as rain, snow, sleet, or hail, depending on the temperature profile of the lower atmosphere. Some of the returned water infiltrates the soil and replenishes groundwater aquifers; the remainder runs off into streams, rivers, and lakes, where it eventually reaches the ocean or sublimates back into the air. The residence time of a water molecule in each reservoir varies from days for atmospheric moisture to thousands of years for deep groundwater and ice.

Photosynthesis converts solar energy into chemical energy stored in carbohydrates, supporting the great majority of life on the planet. In the chloroplasts of plant cells, pigment molecules absorb specific wavelengths of light and use the energy to split water and reduce carbon dioxide. The byproduct is molecular oxygen, which accumulates in the atmosphere and, over geological time, has shifted the chemistry of the surface from reducing to oxidizing. The carbohydrates produced fuel cellular respiration, the inverse process by which most organisms extract energy from food, and they form the structural matter of plant tissues that ultimately feed nearly every terrestrial food web.

The atmosphere is layered according to its temperature profile. The troposphere, nearest the ground, contains most of the mass and nearly all the weather; temperature falls with altitude until the tropopause. Above lies the stratosphere, where temperature rises with height due to absorption of ultraviolet radiation by ozone. Higher still are the mesosphere, where temperature again declines and where meteors typically burn up, and the thermosphere, where rarefied gases are ionized by short-wavelength radiation. The boundaries between layers are not perfectly sharp, and seasonal and latitudinal variations move them up or down by kilometers.

Ocean currents redistribute heat across the planet and moderate climates that would otherwise be more extreme. Surface currents are driven primarily by the prevailing winds, while deep currents follow density gradients set by temperature and salinity. The Atlantic meridional overturning, for instance, carries warm water northward at the surface and returns cold, dense water southward at depth, releasing heat to the air above western Europe along the way. Disruptions to such circulations can have outsized consequences for regional weather and for the timing of seasonal events such as the monsoons.

Mountain ranges form by several distinct processes. Compressional mountains, like the Himalayas, arise at convergent plate boundaries where crustal material is folded, faulted, and uplifted as one plate is driven beneath another. Volcanic mountains, such as those of the Cascade Range, form where rising magma reaches the surface and accumulates as lava and pyroclastic deposits. Block mountains, found in extensional tectonic settings, are produced when blocks of crust are uplifted along normal faults while adjacent blocks subside. Erosion by water, wind, and ice eventually wears mountains down, exposing the metamorphic and igneous rocks that formed at depth.

Soil is the thin layer of weathered mineral matter, organic material, water, and air that supports terrestrial plant life. Its development depends on the parent rock, the climate, the topography, the resident organisms, and the time elapsed since the surface began to weather. Distinct horizons emerge as percolating water carries clays and dissolved minerals downward and as organisms cycle nutrients between layers. The fertility of a given soil reflects the balance among these processes, and management practices that ignore the slow rate at which deep soil regenerates can deplete an agricultural landscape within a few decades.

Glaciers form where annual snowfall exceeds annual melting over long enough periods for the accumulated snow to compress into ice. Once the ice is thick enough, gravity drives it to deform plastically and to slide downslope, carving valleys and ridges and depositing the eroded material along its margins. Continental ice sheets, which currently cover Antarctica and Greenland, store enough water to raise global sea level by tens of meters if entirely melted. Variations in the size of ice sheets over the past several million years have driven repeated cycles of glaciation and interglacial warmth that are reflected in marine sediment cores and in the isotopic composition of ice itself.

Volcanism is the surface expression of magmatic processes operating beneath the crust. Magma rises buoyantly through the lithosphere and erupts in styles that depend on its composition and gas content. Low-silica magmas, common at oceanic hotspots and at mid-ocean ridges, erupt as fluid lava flows that can travel many kilometers from the vent. High-silica magmas, common above subduction zones, are far more viscous and trap dissolved gases that drive explosive eruptions, ejecting ash and pyroclastic material into the atmosphere. The largest such eruptions can affect global climate for years by injecting sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere.

Earthquakes occur when stress accumulated along a fault is released as the rock on either side slips abruptly. The released energy radiates outward as seismic waves, which travel through the interior and along the surface at characteristic speeds determined by the density and elasticity of the rocks they pass through. Body waves arrive first at distant stations, while surface waves arrive later and typically cause more damage to structures. Networks of seismometers worldwide record the patterns of arrivals and infer the location, depth, and magnitude of each event, building a continually refined picture of the structure of the interior.

The carbon cycle moves carbon among the atmosphere, ocean, terrestrial biosphere, and rocks of the crust. Atmospheric carbon dioxide dissolves into surface waters, where it equilibrates with dissolved bicarbonate and carbonate ions. Marine organisms incorporate the carbonate into shells and skeletons that, on death, settle to the seafloor and accumulate as carbonate sediment. Over long timescales, plate motion subducts a portion of these sediments back into the mantle, where some of the carbon is released by volcanic outgassing. Photosynthesis fixes atmospheric carbon into terrestrial biomass, and respiration and decomposition return it to the air. The net balance among these fluxes determines the abundance of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere over geological time.

Weather and climate differ primarily in timescale. Weather refers to the instantaneous and short-term state of the atmosphere: temperature, humidity, wind, precipitation, and cloud cover at a given place and time. Climate refers to the long-term distribution of weather statistics over decades and longer. A region's climate is shaped by its latitude, altitude, proximity to oceans, prevailing wind patterns, and the configuration of nearby landmasses. Changes in any of these factors, whether driven by orbital variations, plate motion, or shifts in atmospheric composition, can alter the statistical distribution and, with it, the kinds of weather that are typical at a given location.

Rivers shape landscapes by eroding the channels they flow through and by depositing the eroded material elsewhere. The transport capacity of a river depends on its discharge and on the slope and roughness of its bed. In their upper reaches, where slopes are steep, rivers tend to incise narrow valleys; in their lower reaches, where slopes flatten, they deposit sediment to form floodplains, deltas, and meanders. The pattern of erosion and deposition is not steady: floods and droughts shift it on timescales of years, while tectonic uplift and climate change shift it on timescales of millennia.

The biosphere has been shaped repeatedly by mass extinction events in which a large fraction of existing species disappeared in a relatively short interval of geological time. The end-Permian event, around 252 million years ago, eliminated most marine genera and reset the trajectory of evolution on land. The end-Cretaceous event, around 66 million years ago, ended the long reign of non-avian dinosaurs and opened ecological space for mammals and birds to diversify. Each such reset alters the composition of the surviving biota and the structure of the food webs that follow, with effects that persist for millions of years.

Wind transports particles ranging from fine dust to coarse sand, depending on the strength of the airflow and the size of the available material. Desert landscapes, where loose sand is abundant and vegetation is sparse, are characterized by mobile dunes whose shapes reflect the prevailing wind direction and the supply of sand. Loess plateaus form downwind of glacial outwash plains, where fine silt deposited by retreating ice was lifted by post-glacial winds and accumulated in thick, fertile blankets. The chemistry and microfossil content of long-traveled dust can record the climate of distant source regions.

Coral reefs are biological structures built by colonial cnidarians, in symbiosis with photosynthetic dinoflagellates, that accumulate carbonate skeletons in shallow tropical waters. The accreted skeletons form three-dimensional habitat for an exceptionally diverse community of fish, invertebrates, and algae. Reef growth is sensitive to water temperature, salinity, light availability, and the chemistry of dissolved carbonate; stresses on any of these can cause the coral animals to expel their symbionts, a phenomenon known as bleaching, after which the colony may recover slowly or die outright depending on the duration of the stress.

Lakes record local climate and ecological history in their sediments. Annually layered varved sediments preserve year-by-year chronologies of pollen, diatoms, ostracods, and geochemical markers. The pollen assemblage reflects the vegetation surrounding the lake at the time of deposition, while the diatom and ostracod assemblages reflect the temperature, depth, and chemistry of the water column. Stable isotope ratios in the carbonate fraction encode the temperature and the isotopic composition of the source precipitation. Together these proxies allow reconstructions of climate variations over thousands to tens of thousands of years.

Crystalline rocks form by cooling and solidification of magma or by recrystallization of pre-existing rocks under elevated pressure and temperature. Igneous rocks that cool slowly at depth develop coarse interlocking crystals visible to the unaided eye; those that cool rapidly at or near the surface develop fine-grained or glassy textures. Metamorphic rocks form when heat, pressure, and chemically active fluids reorganize the mineral assemblage of a parent rock without melting it entirely. The pressure-temperature path recorded in metamorphic minerals can reveal the tectonic history of the region in which they formed.

Lichens are composite organisms formed from the symbiosis of a fungus with one or more photosynthetic partners, usually green algae or cyanobacteria. They occupy a remarkable range of environments, from polar deserts to tropical rainforests, and they often colonize bare rock and tree bark that few other organisms can exploit. The slow growth of many lichen species and their sensitivity to atmospheric pollution make them useful indicators of environmental quality, and the dating of lichen colonies on exposed surfaces can constrain the timing of glacial retreat, landslide, or rock-fall events.

Estuaries are partially enclosed bodies of water where fresh river discharge mixes with salt water from the open sea. The resulting gradients in salinity, temperature, and turbidity support productive ecosystems that act as nurseries for many fish and invertebrate species. Sediment delivered by rivers accumulates in estuaries, building marshes and mudflats that buffer the coast against storm surge and that bury and slowly mineralize large quantities of organic carbon. Tides moderate the freshwater-saltwater interface on short timescales, while seasonal and longer-term variations in river discharge shift it inland or seaward.

Deserts are regions where annual evaporation potential greatly exceeds precipitation. They occur in a range of climatic settings, from cold polar deserts to hot subtropical ones. Subtropical deserts cluster around the descending limbs of the atmospheric Hadley circulation near 30 degrees latitude, where dry air sinks and warms adiabatically. Rain-shadow deserts form on the lee side of major mountain ranges that wring moisture from prevailing winds. Coastal deserts occur where cold ocean currents suppress evaporation from offshore waters, denying nearby land the moisture needed to drive precipitation despite proximity to the sea.

Fossils provide direct evidence of the morphology and distribution of ancient organisms. Preservation depends on the rapid burial of remains in fine-grained sediment that excludes oxygen and decomposers, and on the resistance of the original tissues to dissolution and recrystallization. Hard parts such as bones, teeth, and shells preserve more readily than soft tissues, but rare lagerstätten, with their exceptional preservation, can record the soft anatomy of organisms whose fossil record is otherwise restricted to fragmentary hard parts. The taxonomic affiliations and stratigraphic distribution of fossils are the basis for the relative dating that anchors the geological timescale prior to the development of radiometric methods.

Soils support most of the biological productivity of terrestrial ecosystems, and their long-term management is essential for sustainable land use. Agricultural practices that disturb the soil structure, deplete organic matter, or accelerate erosion can reduce productivity and, in extreme cases, render land barren within a few human generations. Conservation tillage, cover cropping, and careful management of irrigation water can substantially reduce these losses. Restoration of degraded soils typically requires decades of careful intervention, and the recovery of mature topsoil from bare parent rock requires far longer.

Mineral resources are concentrations of useful elements or compounds that can be extracted at a profit under current technology and prices. Ore deposits form through a wide variety of geological processes, including magmatic differentiation, hydrothermal circulation, sedimentary accumulation, and weathering enrichment. The distribution of deposits across the planet reflects long-term plate motion, the configuration of ancient basins, and the chemistry of past oceans and atmospheres. Exhaustion of high-grade deposits drives the development of techniques to recover useful materials from progressively lower-grade ores, with corresponding increases in energy use and environmental footprint.

The atmospheres of the terrestrial planets diverged early in solar-system history despite similar initial compositions. Mercury retains essentially no atmosphere because its low gravity and high temperature have allowed atmospheric gases to escape over time. Venus possesses a thick atmosphere of carbon dioxide that sustains a runaway greenhouse and surface temperatures hot enough to melt lead. Earth retains a more moderate atmosphere of nitrogen, oxygen, and trace gases, modulated by an active hydrological cycle and a biosphere that has rebalanced its composition over geological time. Mars retains a thin carbon-dioxide atmosphere whose pressure varies seasonally as a fraction of the gas freezes out at the winter polar cap.

Glacial-interglacial cycles over the past several million years have followed the rhythms of Earth's orbital variations, with periodic changes in eccentricity, obliquity, and the precession of the equinoxes pacing the advance and retreat of the great northern ice sheets. The amplitude of the climatic response is far larger than the orbital forcing alone would suggest, because feedbacks involving ice albedo, ocean circulation, and the carbon cycle amplify the initial signal. Variations in the strength of these feedbacks, in turn, have changed the periodicity of the cycles between roughly 41,000 and 100,000 years over the past three million.

The continental crust and the oceanic crust differ in composition, thickness, and longevity. Continental crust is dominated by lighter, silica-rich rocks and averages around 35 kilometers in thickness, with much thicker roots beneath active mountain belts. Oceanic crust is denser, basaltic, and roughly seven kilometers thick. Because continental crust is buoyant relative to the mantle, it is rarely subducted; the oldest continental rocks date back nearly four billion years, whereas the oldest in-situ oceanic crust is younger than 200 million years, with all earlier oceanic crust having been returned to the mantle.

Bird migration follows long-established flyways that connect breeding grounds with overwintering ranges. The cues that initiate departure and that guide navigation include the angle and timing of sunlight, the geometry of the geomagnetic field, prominent landscape features, and learned routes passed between generations. Stable isotope signatures preserved in feathers track the geographical sources of food that the bird ate during molt, providing a record of past stopover sites that can be reconstructed long after the migration itself has ended. Changes in the timing and routing of migration in response to shifting climate offer a sensitive measure of how ecosystems respond to longer-term forcing.
